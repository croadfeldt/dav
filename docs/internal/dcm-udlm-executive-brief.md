# UDLM / DCM — Executive Brief

**Making Infrastructure Lifecycle — and the Capability Reasoning Built on It — Machine-Native**

---

## The Problem

Every large organization manages infrastructure through the same broken process. Multiple internal teams — security, standards, networking, storage, compliance, operations — all have a stake in every resource, but no standardized way to participate. Each builds its own integration, maintains its own representation, enforces its own policies through its own mechanisms. The information loop is broken: what was intended, what was requested, what was provisioned, and what actually exists right now are tracked in different systems, different formats, or not tracked at all.

Six major financial services institutions independently asked for the same solution across 2025 — a standardized way to manage infrastructure lifecycle and let internal stakeholders participate through common contracts rather than bespoke integration. Each was building its own version. Independently. Incompatibly. At enormous cost.

## The Approach

The model has a real origin. It began in late 2024 with a US Navy need — managing infrastructure across disconnected ship-and-shore environments, where what was *intended*, *requested*, *realized*, and *actually running* had to stay reconciled without constant connectivity. That problem generalized into a standard.

**UDLM** (Universal Data Lifecycle Model) is an open, wire-compatible data model specification — currently ~55 documents defining how systems represent and exchange infrastructure lifecycle data. Its core abstraction is a four-state lifecycle: **intent** (what was asked for) → **request** (enriched and validated) → **realized** (what was done) → **discovered** (what actually exists). This closes the information loop.

UDLM defines standardized contracts — for providers, policies, governance, observability — so that any internal group can participate in infrastructure lifecycle through the architecture rather than through custom integration. The CISO office injects security policies. The standards body defines resource definitions. The networking team offers services. All through the same contracts, without modifying the control plane. And once lifecycle data is in a common, machine-readable shape, the reasoning built on top of it — capability coverage, maturity, gap analysis, roadmap prioritization — becomes machine-native too, instead of living in slide decks and consultants' heads.

**DCM** (Data Center Management) operationalizes UDLM as a control plane with a policy engine, provider abstraction, and dependency graph. **DAV** (Data Architecture Validator) was built to validate the DCM architecture — it evaluates use cases against the architecture, reasons about capability and maturity, and produces gap analyses and roadmaps — and it represents all of that data in UDLM, exercising the data model in practice.

Capabilities that were not in the original design brief — data sovereignty, federation, cross-domain orchestration — emerged through engagement with customers and partners who brought real requirements that exercised the model's extensibility. The design principles created the conditions; the community's requirements shaped the result. These capabilities are now core tenants of the architecture, refined through collaboration.

## Evidence (snapshot: 2026-06-16)

The reference implementation evaluated **84 use cases** from **6 financial services institutions** against the UDLM/DCM architecture, surfacing **248 gaps** (3 critical, 18 major, 135 moderate) clustered into 11 capability themes across 3 priority tiers. These figures are point-in-time and reproducible — the analysis is a query against the current data, regenerated as use cases or the specification change. The standard's own architectural roadmap was generated by its own tooling — a reproducible query, not a hand-authored document.

The model is running software, not only specification: in May 2026 the first DCM engineering sample was demonstrated end to end by **rehydrating a sovereign workload** — rebuilding a region-constrained environment from stored intent, with sovereignty policy enforced throughout.

Two independently-built lifecycle management platforms — one centralized, one fully federated — were mapped to the same UDLM model, revealing alignment points and architectural tradeoffs that neither team had identified without a shared representation.

The capability model has also proven valuable for **strategic sourcing**: a global systems integrator was crosswalked against all 13 DCM capability domains, producing per-capability sourcing dispositions (Adopt, Uplift, Replace/Build) with a 6-dimension scoring rubric. The result was a combined sourcing view where the technology vendor and the delivery partner are complementary layers — each capability has a single strategic source, with traceable, defensible decisions rather than vendor-by-vendor opinions.

Every core capability in the model was shaped by real organizations' operational needs: service boundaries, automated placement, data center rehydration, path-to-production enforcement. Some requirements mapped to existing primitives; others drove new contracts and refinements. The model grew stronger through each engagement — designed to be extensible, and proven extensible by the community that uses it. Within Red Hat, OSAC (the Open Sovereign AI Cloud) is a potential integration partner: OSAC's strength is provisioning platforms — a realization of UDLM data operationalized by DCM — so the two are better together, giving customers a partner's provisioning capability out of the box, governed and machine-native, at a fraction of the time-to-value of building it themselves. The Cost Management and confidential computing teams are contributing domain-specific capabilities. A semiconductor partner has invested in a dedicated co-engineering lab for multi-customer DCM validation.

## The Vision: Everything as a Service, Working Together

UDLM enables every group that participates in infrastructure lifecycle to **codify their work as a service** — and those services compose naturally to accomplish whatever intent an organization expresses. The networking team offers IP allocation as a service. The security team offers policy validation as a service. The cost management team offers chargeback as a service. Each codifies their expertise into standardized contracts. When someone expresses the intent "I need a production web application," the control plane assembles the right services, routes through the right policies, and fulfills the intent — without any human needing to understand every domain involved.

In practice: a developer requests a production web application in a specific region. The control plane decomposes it into web, app, and database tiers — each fulfilled by a different provider service. Sovereignty policy eliminates providers outside the region. Cost policy selects the cheapest qualifier. Security policy enriches with production hardening. The developer asked once; six services composed; every governance requirement was met without a single approval ticket. The same pattern applies to branch office provisioning, disaster recovery rehydration, drift remediation, and cost-optimized placement — intent expressed, services composed, policies governed, lifecycle tracked.

Governance is structural, not procedural — enforced by policy in the architecture, not by humans remembering to follow a process. AI sits on top of this deterministic layer: it enhances the system (pattern analysis, predictive gap detection, natural language intake, automated capability assessment) while the policy engine retains final authority. The machine-native lifecycle data is what makes AI-driven infrastructure reasoning possible — and what keeps it governed. The integration model is deliberately surface-agnostic: the load-bearing concerns — governance, provenance, and orchestration — live in the contracts and the lifecycle model, and integrations (developer portals, ITSM systems, CI/CD pipelines) attach on top without the core needing to know about them.

## Build With Us

This is a shared foundation being built **with** and **for** the community — to enable and empower organizations to solve their own problems on common ground.

The momentum is real: the institutions that independently converged on this need were convened by the Red Hat FlightPath team in New York at IBM in 2025 to learn from each other, and several are now co-engineering toward a single shared platform rather than each building its own in limited scope. The case for consolidating is concrete — **fewer duplicated engineering efforts**, **faster time-to-market** for new capabilities, and **corporate support** instead of a fragile in-house fork.

Three categories of partnership are already forming:

- **Co-engineering partners** (enterprise customers): bring your use cases and operational models. Shape the standard while it's being formed. The institutions who engage now are co-authors, not consumers.
- **Delivery partners** (systems integrators, managed services providers): crosswalk your capabilities against the DCM model. The common language enables objective, capability-level sourcing decisions that benefit both you and your customers.
- **Technology partners** (Red Hat internal teams, infrastructure vendors): contribute domain-specific capabilities through the same standardized contracts that external partners use.

One institution already took the architecture, pulled it into their own tooling, and ran their own independent analysis. That is the goal: a standard that empowers organizations to solve their own problems on common ground.

### The Destination

The end goal: a single unified control plane and data model to manage the lifecycle and configuration of everything in a data center and enterprise — resources, processes, policies, services, cost, compliance. Two paths lead there: integrate UDLM into all Red Hat products (each becomes a provider speaking the common language), or position DCM as the control plane that binds them together. Both paths serve the same purpose — unified, governed lifecycle management — and both are compatible with broader platform initiatives. The model is domain-agnostic by design: the same primitives that manage infrastructure have already extended to software delivery and capability reasoning, and the entity-type family mechanism ensures new domains extend the model without changing the substrate.

**The destination is CNCF.** UDLM is being built with the intent to submit to the Cloud Native Computing Foundation as a vendor-neutral, community-governed standard. The community we build now becomes the multi-organization foundation that makes that submission credible.
